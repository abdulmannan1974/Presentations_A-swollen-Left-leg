# Before You Tick the Box

Grand round · 9 October 2026 · Dr Abdul Mannan

21 main slides, paced for 15 minutes; two optional reference slides. Arrow keys reveal each step before advancing.

## 1. Title · 00:00–00:20

Salaam and good afternoon. The title is the whole talk: before you tick the box. Fifteen minutes on one of the most common reflexes in medicine: a clot appears, a thrombophilia screen goes off. My aim is simple. By the end you should know what an acute clot needs from you today, and which of those tests can wait, or never needs sending.

## 2. The reflex · 00:20–01:05

Picture the take. An adult with a first, left-sided iliofemoral DVT and no obvious trigger. Click: the form fills itself. Seven boxes, one stamp, and everyone feels productive. Ask the room: which result do you need before treating this clot? Pause. Click: none of this routine panel should delay treatment. Today we assess the limb, lungs and bleeding risk, confirm the extent and anticoagulate. Keep this fictional patient in mind; we will come back to them.

## 3. The textbook · 01:05–01:25

Dacie and Lewis makes the point directly: routine testing during the acute event is not recommended. The clot, acute illness and our treatment can distort the result. This is about the reflex panel; rare emergencies such as purpura fulminans need urgent specialist investigation.

## 4. The five jobs · 01:25–02:00

These five jobs organise the work, not a rigid queue. If life or limb is threatened, escalate immediately and assess and treat in parallel. Confirm the event and extent, identify its context, assess limb and lungs, weigh bleeding risk and anticoagulate. The history informs the later duration discussion; it does not replace that discussion. Click: the routine thrombophilia screen is missing because it is not a prerequisite for any of these immediate actions.

## 5. Confirm it · 02:00–02:40

NICE NG158 in one picture. Two-level Wells after a history and examination. Click: if DVT is likely, ultrasound within four hours; if you cannot scan in time, D-dimer and interim anticoagulation, scan within 24 hours. Click: if unlikely, D-dimer first, with an age-adjusted cut-off over 50. Click: baseline bloods are FBC, renal and liver function, PT and APTT, and you do not wait for them. And once the clot is confirmed, we start one tool: the DVT checklist.

## 6. The DVT checklist · 02:40–03:25

This is our one-page DVT checklist. It makes the history uniform and plans follow-up. Click through the same questions: where is the clot, and is it the first? What triggered it? Are there cancer symptoms or a persistent risk? What could bleed, and which anticoagulant is suitable? Finally, who reviews the patient, and when? The context informs the duration review alongside bleeding risk and patient preference. Notice there is no reflex thrombophilia box.

## 7. Name the trigger · 03:25–04:05

The clinical context is one of the most useful things we establish. Major surgery is not the same as a weak trigger. Pregnancy, the puerperium and hormonal therapy count as provoking circumstances in NICE terminology, but their implications differ. Persistent risks and previous events matter too. At three months, balance recurrence risk, bleeding and the patient’s preferences. The checklist makes the history consistent. Cancer investigation should follow symptoms, signs and the baseline assessment, rather than a reflex whole-body screen.

## 8. Is the limb at risk? · 04:05–05:15

Watch the clot climb. First the calf; then popliteal and femoral: proximal DVT. Next common femoral and iliac: iliofemoral. The internal iliac branches drain the pelvis. Finally the inferior vena cava: iliocaval disease. This staged drawing is a teaching illustration, not a claim that all DVTs begin in the calf. A blue, tense, very painful leg, especially with sensory or motor change, needs emergency vascular assessment. For symptomatic iliofemoral disease, discuss selected early treatment with vascular or interventional radiology. NICE’s catheter-directed thrombolysis criteria are on screen; iliac involvement alone does not mandate intervention. In left-sided disease, consider compression of the left common iliac vein by the right common iliac artery in the appropriate context.

## 9. Is the life at risk? · 05:15–06:00

For PE, decide whether life is at risk. Haemodynamic instability means immediate senior and critical-care involvement, UFH and consideration of reperfusion. Stable patients with RV dysfunction and raised troponin at higher clinical risk need close monitoring; deterioration may trigger rescue reperfusion. A low PESI score alone does not make a patient low risk if RV dysfunction or biomarkers are abnormal. Outpatient treatment requires validated selection and a proper follow-up plan. Routine primary systemic thrombolysis is not for haemodynamically stable PE. This is the ESC risk framework, with NICE management advice, not a substitute for the full local PE pathway.

## 10. What anticoagulation does · 06:00–06:55

Watch the first track: untreated, the clot grows. Click: anticoagulation suppresses further coagulation, reducing propagation and embolisation. It does not melt the thrombus. Click: endogenous fibrinolysis and remodelling do the clearing. That process is variable and can remain incomplete, so residual clot is not automatically treatment failure. New or worsening symptoms still deserve assessment. NICE recommends at least three months for proximal DVT or PE, with a planned review. Thrombolysis and mechanical clot removal are separate treatments for selected patients.

## 11. Bleeding risk and extension · 06:55–07:45

Before treatment, assess recent bleeding, haemoglobin, platelets, renal function and interacting medicines. Then give a clear safety-net. Worsening swelling, new pain, a blue or cold leg, chest pain, breathlessness or collapse need reassessment. Major bleeding, melaena or a severe new headache also need urgent assessment. Suspected treatment failure needs objective confirmation and a review of adherence, dose, interactions and underlying causes. Involve the treating team before changing anticoagulant strategy; worsening symptoms are not a reason to send a routine thrombophilia panel.

## 12. The reflex · 07:45–07:55

So the patient is anticoagulated. Now the reflex.

## 13. What is in the screen · 07:55–08:25

Quick map. Heritable traits: Factor V Leiden and the prothrombin variant are common in Europeans and raise first-event risk three to five times. Protein C, S and antithrombin deficiencies are rare. Acquired: APS, PNH and MPN. Click: the BSH reminds us that the relative risk for recurrence is lower than for a first event, which is why a positive trait rarely changes what we do.

## 14. The clot rewrites the result · 08:25–09:30

The same patient can appear deficient, normal or even high, depending on when and how you test. Click through the columns. Acute illness, recent thrombosis and acquired disease can lower these proteins; recent clot especially affects S and antithrombin, whereas protein C may fall in DIC or severe sepsis. Heparin may lower antithrombin. Warfarin lowers both C and S. DOACs can falsely raise clot-based C/S activity and drug-matched antithrombin assays, hiding a deficiency. The method matters: chromogenic C and free S antigen avoid some direct drug effects. BSH says test these deficiencies, if indicated, after at least three months of treatment. Ongoing interference still needs an agreed plan; three months is not a washout.

## 15. Lupus anticoagulant: the drug in the tube · 09:30–10:35

Lupus anticoagulant is a functional laboratory phenomenon: we time phospholipid-dependent clotting reactions. Click: the patient’s anticoagulant is in the tube as well. Click: it can produce false-positive or false-negative patterns, depending on drug, concentration and reagent. A normal routine PT or APTT does not reliably prove the sample is drug free. Click: BSH suggests avoiding LA testing while anticoagulated or within three months of an acute thrombosis. If the question matters sooner, call the laboratory; do not stop doses to get the result. Anticardiolipin and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I are different, solid-phase tests. They can start an indicated APS work-up, but a positive result needs context and persistence. Negative solid-phase antibodies do not exclude isolated LA. Confirmed APS may change anticoagulant choice, particularly in triple-positive disease.

## 16. Two questions before any test · 10:35–11:30

Two questions: can I trust the result today, and will it change care? Click: routine C/S/AT and LA need deferral or a laboratory plan. An abnormal acute result does not establish an inherited deficiency. Click: DNA tests for Factor V Leiden and prothrombin are unaffected by anticoagulants, but rarely change immediate treatment and are not a routine panel. Click: solid-phase antibodies can start an indicated APS work-up. JAK2 and PNH can matter in the right site and phenotype. They still need interpretation and a named reviewer. Click: can be sent is not the same as should be sent.

## 17. Who to test · 11:30–12:35

Start from the clinical problem. A major transient trigger does not usually call for thrombophilia testing. Unprovoked VTE may justify an APS work-up because the drug choice can change; BSH also suggests this after minor triggers. NICE is narrower, focusing on a planned stop. For unusual-site thrombosis, consider aPL and an MPN or PNH question. JAK2 can be relevant in unexplained splanchnic or cerebral venous thrombosis despite a normal count. PNH needs the appropriate cytopenic or haemolytic phenotype. Adult arterial thrombosis and stroke do not call for a routine inherited panel. These are UK BSH/NICE principles; ASH 2023 is broader in selected nonsurgical/hormonal settings, conditionally on largely very-low-certainty evidence. Rare emergencies such as purpura fulminans or suspected catastrophic APS require specialist testing.

## 18. When the reflex harms · 12:35–13:15

Four ways the reflex hurts: a false protein S deficiency label follows the family; a drug-affected LA becomes a false APS diagnosis, or true APS is missed; a normal panel gives false reassurance; and early tests need repeating. Click: routine inherited results usually do not change the drug, intensity or monitoring of acute treatment. A specific antithrombin or other urgent specialist question is different. Name the decision before requesting the test.

## 20. Back to Tuesday · 13:55–14:35

Back to our patient. Confirmed, unprovoked after careful history, no limb threat, bleeding assessed and anticoagulation started. Because the clot is symptomatic and iliofemoral, discuss selected treatment and possible iliac compression. Send aCL and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I only for the defined APS question. Plan valid LA and repeat positive antibodies at least twelve weeks apart. A single positive is not APS, and negative solid-phase tests do not exclude isolated LA. At three months, recurrence risk, bleeding and preference guide the duration decision—not a reflex panel.
